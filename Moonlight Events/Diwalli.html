Diwali

Diwali, also known as the Festival of Lights, is one of the most significant festivals in Hindu culture, celebrated with great enthusiasm across India and by the Indian diaspora worldwide. It usually falls between October and November, marking the victory of light over darkness and good over evil.
During Diwali, people illuminate their homes with oil lamps, candles, and colorful lights, creating a mesmerizing spectacle. The festival symbolizes the triumph of Lord Rama over the demon king Ravana, as depicted in the Hindu epic Ramayana. It also marks the return of Lord Rama, his wife Sita, and brother Lakshmana to their kingdom Ayodhya after 14 years of exile.
Apart from lighting lamps, Diwali celebrations involve various rituals, including prayers to deities such as Lakshmi, the goddess of wealth and prosperity. People clean their homes and decorate them with rangoli (colorful patterns made with colored powders) and floral decorations. Families come together to exchange gifts, share festive meals, and burst firecrackers, adding to the joyous atmosphere.
Diwali is not just a religious festival; it is a time for renewal, reflection, and the strengthening of bonds with family and friends. It promotes unity and a sense of goodwill among people of different communities and backgrounds, making it a truly inclusive celebration of light, love, and happiness.



History

Diwali, or Deepavali, originated as an ancient Hindu festival marking the end of the harvest season. Its historical and mythological significance includes the return of Lord Rama to Ayodhya after defeating Ravana, symbolizing the victory of good over evil. Diwali also celebrates the defeat of demon Narakasura by Lord Krishna and the triumph of the Pandavas in the Mahabharata. It's a time for lighting lamps, worshiping Goddess Lakshmi for prosperity, and fostering spiritual renewal. Over time, it has become a multi-dimensional festival celebrated with family gatherings, feasting, and fireworks, symbolizing light, hope, and joy.
